# Intent and the page plan

How to decide which pages a site needs, which page targets which search terms, and how the pages
link to each other.

## Gather the demand

For each product, service or topic, collect:

1. Search terms with monthly volume for the target country, from a keyword tool or the search
   console of the site itself. Record the source and the date next to every number.
2. The buyer questions people actually ask: Google's "People also ask" boxes, related searches,
   sales and support conversations, community threads.
3. The current top 10 organic results for each candidate term, as domains, in the target country.

If you cannot get real data, say so and ask the person for it. A plan built on guessed volumes
sends pages after terms nobody searches.

If you must build before data arrives, choose a provisional primary term from the business's
category, location and main offer as people would type them ("sourdough bakery portland",
"wholesale bakery austin"). Mark it provisional in the ownership table and in your report, write
no volume next to it, and check it against real data before launch.

## Build the term ownership table

One row per page. Each search term appears in exactly one row.

| Page | Primary term | Secondary terms |
|---|---|---|
| `/` | the category term for the business | close variants with heavy SERP overlap |
| `/service-a` | the service's own term | its "agency", "company", "services" variants |
| `/guides/how-to-x` | the question as people type it | follow-up questions |

Rules:

- A page has one primary term. Its title, H1 and opening sentence use it.
- Secondary terms are close variants that share most of their top 10 with the primary term.
- If a term fits two pages, run the overlap test and give it to one of them.
- Informational terms ("how to", "what is", "vs") go to guides. Commercial terms ("agency",
  "services", "pricing", "near me") go to service and conversion pages.

## The SERP overlap test

Search engines show you how they group intents. If two queries return many of the same domains in
their top 10, the engine treats them as one need, and two of your pages targeting them will
compete. If they share almost nothing, one page cannot satisfy both.

1. Take the top 10 organic results for term A and term B (same country, same device).
2. Reduce each result to its domain. Ignore ads, AI answer boxes and video carousels.
3. Count the domains that appear in both lists.

| Shared | Decision |
|---|---|
| 4 or more | Same intent. One page owns both. If two of your pages target them, retarget one. |
| 2 to 3 | Related. One page can carry the second term as a secondary, if the page's content covers it. |
| 0 to 1 | Different intents. Separate pages are justified. |

Watch for ambiguous terms. A short term can mean something unrelated to your business (a "geo
agency" can be a government mapping office). Check that the top 10 is about your topic before you
target it.

## Retarget instead of compete

When an existing page overlaps a more important page:

1. Find a nearby term that no page owns and that matches what the weaker page already says.
2. Change the weaker page's title, H1, opening and internal anchors to that term.
3. Keep the URL if it still describes the page. Changing a URL costs history and needs a 301.
4. Link the weaker page to the stronger one with the stronger page's term as anchor text.

Example: a guide titled "How to choose a bakery supplier" shares 4 of 10 domains with the
homepage's "wholesale bakery supplier". Retarget the guide to "how to choose a wholesale bread
supplier" questions and let the homepage own the commercial term.

## Read the SERP shape before choosing a format

Look at what kind of page wins each term, because the engine is telling you what format satisfies
it:

- Service pages win: build a service page.
- Lists and roundups win: a service page will struggle; consider a guide or a list page with a
  stated method.
- How-to guides and definitions win: the term is informational; it belongs to a guide.
- Tools win: the person wants to do something now; a tool page fits.

Also note who wins: if the top 10 is all large, long-established domains, a new site needs a more
specific term first.

## Hubs, spokes and anchor text

- Each topic has a hub page (often the service page or a guide index) and spoke pages (guides,
  comparisons, tools).
- Every spoke links to its hub, and the hub links to every spoke.
- Each guide links to the commercial page it supports, so the guide's authority flows to the page
  that sells the work.
- Anchor text is the target page's own primary term, written naturally into a sentence. Avoid
  "click here" and avoid using another page's term as an anchor to the wrong page.
- Keep every indexable page within two or three clicks of the homepage. Every inner page is linked
  from at least three other pages, including its hub (the homepage is exempt). A page no other page links to (an orphan) is hard for crawlers to find and
  rarely ranks.

## Example: a fictional bakery

This example is invented to show the method. Larkspur Oven is a fictional bakery in Austin.

| Page | Primary term | Secondary | Why separate |
|---|---|---|---|
| `/` | bakery in austin | austin bakery, artisan bakery austin | Heavy overlap between these |
| `/wholesale` | wholesale bakery austin | bread supplier for restaurants | 1 of 10 shared with the homepage term |
| `/custom-cakes` | custom cakes austin | birthday cakes austin | Different SERP: cake shops and galleries |
| `/guides/sourdough-starter` | how to make a sourdough starter | sourdough starter ratio | Informational |
| `/guides/sourdough-vs-yeast-bread` | sourdough vs yeast bread | | Comparison intent |

The two guides link to the homepage with the anchor "bakery in Austin" where it reads naturally,
and the wholesale page links to the custom cakes page with "custom cakes in Austin".
